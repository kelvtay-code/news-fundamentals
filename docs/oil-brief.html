Overall Market Summary
The single most consequential sentence in today's package was spoken on Iranian state television, not printed in an assessment. Central bank governor Abdolnaser Hemmati, asked about oil revenue: "We have restrictions. There is no doubt. All our neighbouring countries are the same. We speak with the Qataris, Iraqis… their incomes have become close to zero from their [oil] exports. For us, too, this has happened. We don't do [oil] exports. This is a reality." Commodities at Sea corroborates it: the last waterborne Iranian crude shipped in the first week of July, and Iranian crude on water has fallen to 90.53mn barrels from 126.3mn on 18 July — with 64.8mn of that still sitting inside the Middle East Gulf, unable to leave. China's Shandong independents confirm the physical consequence: "There is simply no cargo to be offered"; "We have nothing to trade for the past month." Iranian Light's discount to Brent has narrowed to about $2/b from about $4/b in early August — scarcity pricing in a sanctioned barrel.

That is why the Dubai inversion not only held but deepened. October cash Dubai traded up to $95.60/b against an ICE October Brent settle of $93.78 — medium sour Gulf crude roughly $1.8/b above the world's light sweet benchmark for a second consecutive session. The cash differential to same-month futures rose 90¢ to +$12.40/b, a fresh intramonth high, and the Dubai futures Sep/Oct spread jumped 81¢ to $4.05/b, the highest since 18 May. In the window, sixteen partials cleared and Mercuria bought all sixteen, walking the range from $95.50 to $95.60 — and then Unipec declared a physical cargo of October Oman to Mercuria on the convergence of 20 partials. Paper became a delivery obligation. Against that, the sell side finally moved: ADNOC issued its ninth tender since the war began, offering Upper Zakum, Das Blend and Umm Lulu for October–November loading, and India's MRPL is seeking 1–2mn barrels for late-October delivery.

The S&P Global chokepoint webinar, new to this package, finally quantifies the whole dislocation instead of describing it session by session. Strait of Hormuz transits are averaging 16 per day month-to-date against 28 per day in July; Bab el-Mandeb 35 per day against 41. Middle East Gulf crude exports have collapsed to roughly 8mn b/d from about 18mn pre-war — and critically, the composition has changed: ship-to-ship loadings in the Gulf of Oman now dominate, running about 3.2mn b/d of an August total near 3.5mn b/d, with new loadings from inside Hormuz ports almost nil. Transits via the Omani coast route have "fizzled out"; the majority of recent crossings are either "dark" or via the Iranian-prescribed route. Saudi Arabia has built a third conduit — Yanbu to Egypt, into SUMED, out at Sidi Kerir — sending record reloaded volumes to the Mediterranean and Baltic in August. And the freight arithmetic is brutal: rerouting Yanbu–Singapore away from Bab el-Mandeb adds 28 days and roughly 2.5× the voyage duration.

The freight board is now the cleanest single expression of the war, and it moved again today. A Sinokor VLCC fixed PG–East at w759 against Irini N Lemos at w190 for Fujairah–East — a fourfold penalty for loading inside the strait rather than outside it, wider than yesterday's w570/w185. The Platts Global VLCC index stands at $420,747/day, up 53% on the month; PG–China 270kt is $119.54/mt against a five-year average of $20.32, and PG–UK/Continent 140kt $135.40 against $29.50. Simultaneously the Black Sea has become the second war zone: Black Sea–Med 135kt is up 114% on the month to $68.79/mt and the additional war-risk premium for Suezmaxes and Aframaxes there is $2.90/b against a $1.26 five-year average. Teekay's CEO on the record: attacks are now occurring in "three separate regions that are vital to the global oil trade."

And Washington escalated the financial front overnight. Trump on Truth Social: "Today, I am announcing the MOST CRUSHING ECONOMIC OPERATION EVER TAKEN AGAINST ANY COUNTRY… ANY country that allows its financial institutions, businesses, airports, or government entities to provide any type of lifeline to Iran will itself face TREMENDOUS Economic Consequences." The UAE responded by halting all trade, commercial exchanges and financial transactions with Iran. TD Securities' read, quoted in the package: "Negotiations on hold for weeks and a shift toward economic pressure suggests crude flows in the market will remain critically tight… the fundamental landscape is already convincingly bullish." Brent settled up $2.16 for a fifth straight higher close — the flat price has finally started responding, which is why the Atlantic leg is upgraded today.

Down the barrel, the day was a broad reversal — and two of this brief's own kill-switches fired. Asian gasoline lost both its named disqualifiers in the same session: a seller returned (SKEISG offering 100kb at $113.60/b, and Platts set the assessment off it) and the naphtha premium fell $2.75 to +$24.97/b, three cents under the $25.00 threshold, with the physical crack collapsing $2.60/b to $21.45–21.50 and the RBOB–Brent crack to $42.29 from $47.40. European HSFO lost its squeeze: the Rotterdam 3.5% barge fell a second time and much harder, −$23.75, dragging the delivered bunker down $27 with it. Both calls are cut on their own stated rules rather than on judgement — which is the entire point of naming the rules in advance.

The middle of the barrel produced the day's most bearish physical print, and it came from a tender. S-Oil's 600,000-barrel prompt gasoil sell tender cleared at a discount of $2.00–$2.50/b to the August MOPS average — against September-loading North Asian cargoes that had been trading at premiums of around plus $2s/b. That is a swing of roughly $4–4.50/b in clearing level, attributed by a Singapore trader to "prompt cargoes, and no more demand," compounded by difficulty chartering MR tonnage. The Singapore Sep/Oct gasoil spread duly narrowed to $6.00/b from $6.69, and the cash differential fell a sixth consecutive session to +$4.37/b, though the decline decelerated sharply (−16¢ against −48¢). The offsetting bid: the MOC bid stack rose to MOPS+$4.00 from +$3.50 with four bids against four offers and the gap narrowing to 80¢ from $2.50, and US ULSD stocks fell a third straight week to 95.724mn barrels, about 15mn below the five-year average.

Fuel oil split cleanly by hemisphere, and Asia won decisively. Singapore 380 CST rose $9.65 to $621.55, the cash differential rose $3.60 to +$36.99/mt (a third straight gain, highest since 6 May), the Sep/Oct spread widened to $40/mt, and — the print that settles the argument — the 380 CST crack against Dated Brent turned positive at +58¢/b from +3¢, its first positive reading since end-May. The two-session $6 bid-offer standoff broke upward, clearing at MOPS+$40 above the standing +$39 offer. Physical corroboration arrived from South China, where "all of South China ports have little HSFO, vessels are taking fuel in Hong Kong now" and Xiamen has no availability with August stocks exhausted. The bear flags remain honest and named: Fujairah heavy distillates are still up 49% on the week at 5.35mn barrels, and Pakistan has approved roughly 185,000 mt of fuel oil exports for August.

What would change all of this, in order of consequence: ① 1 September — Iraq's trader-export liberalisation with its 30%-discount mechanism, still the only dated event capable of breaking the Dubai inversion; ② the supply-return schedule, where the webinar now gives hard numbers — 1.54mn b/d of Middle East refinery run loss is Hormuz-driven and reverses the moment transits normalise, against 1.33mn b/d tied to physically damaged assets (Satorp 460kb/d at 60% utilisation, Mina Al-Ahmadi 346kb/d at zero, Sitra 405kb/d at 35%, Ruwais West 417kb/d at 40%, Jazan 400kb/d shut), with S&P modelling gradual recovery only from September onward; and ③ Russia, where Ukrainian strikes have taken 40% of refining capacity offline — 2.79mn b/d — pushing crude runs to 3.9mn b/d, the lowest since March 2005, with the Taneco refinery hit again on 19–20 August in the sixteenth attack this month and Moscow now importing gasoline.

Practically: more than half of the bullish product-scarcity case in Asia and the Middle East is a freight-and-transit story, and freight-driven premia decay far faster than damage-driven ones. That is the single best argument for why none of this brief's Asian product calls except HSFO are held at HIGH, and why every one of them carries a mid-September expiry.
