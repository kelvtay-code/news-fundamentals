3  |  Geopolitics — A Broken Bypass Is Routing Saudi Oil Back Through Hormuz
Petroline has been shut for five days with no update from Riyadh. The consequences are now visible in ship tracks: Saudi tankers are loading inside the Gulf again, cargoes out of Egypt have been cancelled, and European refiners are scrambling. At the same time, Russia and the US are both talking about restricting diesel exports.
🚨 Saudi exports: Sidi Kerir out, Hormuz in
ROUTING SHIFT
  • Nine crude cargoes from Egypt’s Sidi Kerir, about 8 mn bbl, were cancelled after Petroline closed. Buyers included Orlen, Moeve, Hellenic Petroleum and Petroineos. Two Suezmaxes and five Aframaxes were terminated; one VLCC and one Suezmax inquiry withdrawn.
  • Sidi Kerir is fed by SUMED from the Red Sea, and had become Saudi Arabia’s key export hub after the Houthis declared a blockade on Saudi shipping through Bab al-Mandab on July 20.
  • Three Saudi-operated VLCCs crossed Hormuz into the Gulf on Sept 15; at least eight in ten days. Hormuz traffic overall fell to 12 ships from a revised 19.
  • Orlen contracted 16 replacement cargoes since Sept 11 from Norway, the UK, Algeria, Kazakhstan, Azerbaijan and the Americas. Aramco normally covers 45% of Orlen’s crude.
  • Saxo’s Hansen: Petroline “had become a critical bypass … recently carrying an estimated 4–5 million b/d. Its closure has therefore removed one of the most important safety valves in the global oil system.”
  • Saudi production was 6.35 mn b/d in August, below Petroline’s 7 mn b/d capacity.
What it means. Saudi barrels are not disappearing; they are changing route from the Red Sea back to Hormuz. That is bearish for Med freight (fewer Sidi Kerir liftings) and bullish for Gulf-origin VLCC demand. It is also a quiet signal that Hormuz is workable enough for Aramco to commit ships — which I have to weigh against my Gulf-dependent positions.
