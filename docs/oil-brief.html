Overall Market Summary
The single most important number in today's package is a spread that no longer exists in its normal sign. Cash Dubai for October traded up to $93.59/b and the Dubai and Oman cash differentials both firmed $2.97/b on the day to an $11.50/b premium over same-month Dubai futures, month-to-date highs. ICE October Brent settled at $91.62. Medium sour Gulf crude is now printing roughly two dollars a barrel ABOVE the world's light sweet benchmark. A week ago the Pricescore had Brent/Dubai at $8.13; on Monday's data it was $4.11; today it is negative. This is the terminal expression of the thesis this brief has been building for three sessions: the war is not a flat-price event, it is a grade-availability event, and the barrel that cannot get out of the Gulf is the sour one.

The chokepoint data explains the mechanism and it got worse, not better. Hormuz transits fell back to 11 ships on 18 August from a revised 15 on 16 August. One of them was a Saudi crude cargo aboard Sinokor's Singapore Prosperity VLCC bound for Fujairah — and Saudi Arabia has largely rerouted its crude to Red Sea ports precisely to avoid the strait. Sinokor now has close to 50 tankers in the Middle East running a shuttle pattern between Gulf ports and the Gulf of Oman: that is not trade, it is a relay. Meanwhile Bab al-Mandab crossings surged to 51 ships, the highest since 30 July — but oil and LPG tankers are only 32% of those crossings, down from 35% in July and 39% in June, and the Houthis declared a maritime embargo on Saudi shipping on 20 July. The Red Sea is getting busier with everything except oil. Forty-nine ships crossed the US blockade line in the Arabian Sea, 35 of them westbound. Japan Weekly supplies the piece that makes the export data unreliable: "many tankers now turning off their transponders to avoid being targeted" — the latest week's Middle East export numbers are therefore understated by construction, and S&P is only publishing through 9 August as a result.

Against that, the largest bearish datapoint in the package is Iraqi, and it is a policy decision rather than a barrel. Iraq's council of ministers has approved oil exports through global and domestic trading companies effective 1 September, ending SOMO's fifty-four-year monopoly, together with authorisation to buy crude at a 30% discount to the SOMO or budget price, new export routes, and rehabilitation of the IT2A/IT1 pumping stations on the Iraq–Turkey line at 300,000 b/d. Iraq pumped 2.6mn b/d in July against 3.22mn in February, and Basrah Medium was assessed at $63.48/b on 18 August against a 7 April high of $110.65. Set that against Dubai at $93.59 and the picture is unambiguous: there is a very large pool of stranded, deeply discounted Iraqi medium sour that has just been handed a commercial distribution channel. That is the single named threat to the Dubai HIGH conviction, and it has a date on it.

The US balance data returned today after a three-session gap and it is internally contradictory in a way that matters. Commercial crude built 4.41mn barrels to 428.8mn — a third straight counter-seasonal build, an eleven-week high, and above the five-year average for the first time since 1 May. But the SPR drew 5.27mn to 293.4mn, the twenty-first consecutive weekly draw, cumulatively −122mn barrels, and the lowest level since December 1982 — so total US crude actually fell 860,000 barrels. Cushing drew 1.31mn to 21.25mn, 25.1% below the five-year average, at 26% storage utilisation. And the refining system is running flat out: utilisation 97.2%, net crude inputs 17.4mn b/d — the highest since September 2019 — with the only planned maintenance in the country being Monroe's Trainer refinery, because refiners are deferring turnarounds to chase cracks. The US is not long crude; it is drawing down the strategic reserve to feed a refining system running above design, and the commercial build is where that shows up. CERA forecasts a 4.41mn barrel draw for the week to 21 August as imports fall to 5.8mn b/d and exports rise to 4.5mn.

The product side of that is a distillate market at five-year lows and a gasoline market that already looks like October. US distillate stocks drew 1.53mn to 105.6mn barrels, 10.4mn below last year and 12.8% under the seasonal average, with PADD 1 and PADD 3 setting new five-year lows for the second week of August. The US average ULSD crack improved $10.38/b in a week, Chicago's by $12.12 against WTI, and the composite net refining margin reached $44.95/b — thirty dollars above last year and $33.40 above 2024. Gasoline is the quieter but arguably tighter story: total stocks are 14.2mn barrels below year-ago and, in CERA's own words, have "reached levels not typically seen until the fall refinery outage season" because refiners are maximising distillate yield into the global diesel shortage. PADD 5 drew 1.42mn barrels to 28.11mn and West Coast retail is $5.21/gal against a $4.18 national average, up 28.6% year on year.

Asia's light end is where that tightness is most cleanly priced, and for the second consecutive session it is bid-only. The FOB Singapore 92 RON assessment settled at $115.22/b and was set on a Vitol BID at $115.50 — 28¢ above the print — with no offers and no trades, while PTT withdrew every one of its eight offers across 92, 95 and 97 RON for a second day. Both cracks firmed intraday against the previous assessment: the physical 92 RON crack was pegged $24.40–24.45/b against a $23.29 print, the swaps crack $21.25–21.30 against $20.31, and the US RBOB–Brent crack $47.60 against $46.08. An assessment set on a bid above the print, with the largest regional seller refusing to show an offer for a second day, is the highest-quality bullish signal the MOC produces. The named risk arrived in the same package: China's 320,000 b/d Shenghong refining and petrochemical complex has been restarting since 17 August after turnaround, debottlenecking and quality-upgrade work.

The middle of the barrel is now splitting three ways, not two. Asian gasoil failed its re-upgrade test outright — cash differential down a fifth straight session to +$4.53/b, no MOC clearance, a $2.50/b unfilled bid-offer gap, and a Sep/Oct spread pegged lower at $6.60. Asian jet is worse and structurally so: a seventh consecutive bid-less session, a lone Vitol offer at MOPS+$2.50, S-Oil offering 600,000 barrels for end-August loading, and the September regrade at −$4.25/b, down $1.10 in two sessions as China's August export quota is guided above 2.2mn mt versus an initial ~2.0mn expectation. And Europe — the sink that was absorbing both — stalled: every European jet assessment fell $12.75/mt, the Rotterdam gasoil barge fell $11.25, and the Gasoil EFS narrowed $7.43 to −$103.02/mt, moving against Europe for the first time in the series. The clearing route for Chinese kerosene just got more expensive to use — which is bearish Asia and, at the margin, bearish Europe too.

Fuel oil produced the day's most instructive dislocation and it is not the one in the headline. Singapore 380 CST HSFO rose $3.92 to $611.90 with the cash differential at $33.01/mt at the 18 August close, the highest since 6 May, and the physical bid stack built to MOPS+$33 (PetroChina) against a lone Exxon FSU offer at +$39, with no trade for a second session. But in Europe the barge and the bunker moved in opposite directions on the same day: 3.5% FOB Rotterdam barge −$13.50 to $541.75, while the Rotterdam delivered 380 CST bunker rose $42.00 to $618, Antwerp +$42, Dunkirk +$42, Hamburg +$40. Traders describe "extremely long waiting lines at HSFO loading racks in Rotterdam and Antwerp" and suppliers unable to quote delivery dates despite steady bulk HSFO arriving from South America and Iraq on open arbitrage. The cargo is available; the barge slot is not. That is a logistics squeeze, not a molecule squeeze, and logistics squeezes resolve on a shorter clock. The offsetting bear on the Asian leg: Fujairah heavy distillates jumped 49% (+1.753mn bbl) to 5.35mn, the largest single inventory build anywhere in the package.

LPG is downgraded today and the reason is a record. US propane and propylene stocks rose 2.039mn barrels to 107.013mn — the highest since the EIA series began in January 2015 — against a CERA forecast of just 781,000, with PADD 2 up 5% to 26.154mn ahead of crop-drying season and PADD 3 at 67.819mn. Year on year that is +17.3%. Against it: zero Asian trades at the close for a third session, PG–Japan VLGC freight easing to $217/mt from $218.50, and the Saudi CP Sep/Oct backwardation narrowing to $25/mt from $29. FOB Arab Gulf differentials to CP sat unchanged at −$10/mt for both propane and butane. The flat-price gain to a three-week high was crude-led and nothing else. The one genuinely bullish input — the propane/naphtha spread widening to −$83.25/mt from −$79, improving LPG cracking economics — is a naphtha-bearish datapoint dressed up as an LPG-bullish one.

What would change all of this, in order of consequence: ① Iraq's 1 September trader-export liberalisation actually placing discounted Basrah into Asia — the one thing that can break the Dubai inversion; ② the return schedule, which now carries two live datapoints on each side — bearish: Shenghong 320 kb/d restarting from 17 August and Thailand's Rayong Olefins restarting a 900,000 mt/yr cracker mid-to-late September; bullish: Aramco's 400,000 b/d Jazan still offline through at least end-August after the late-July Houthi strike, and ENEOS Marifu's sole 128,000 b/d CDU down since 7 August on technical glitches, leaving 188,000 b/d — 6% of Japanese capacity — offline; and ③ the datasets that remain missing — no FGE Weekly and no Vortexa for a second session, and PAJ product stocks suspended since 21 March, which means every Asian product-inventory statement in this brief rests on Fujairah and Singapore data alone. That gap is flagged, not assumed away.
