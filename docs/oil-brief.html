The dirty freight closure, honestly.

I wrote the stop as “a Hormuz transit print above 18 ships.” It printed 22, I closed, and the next session the AG–Far East VLCC rate printed an all-time high of $161.93/mt while transits fell back to 15.

The temptation is to call this a bad stop. It was not. The stop did its job perfectly: it protected me from a position whose driver I could not read. What it exposes is a different error — I wrote the invalidation on transit counts, but the position was in freight rates. Those are related, not identical. Transits measure how many ships are willing to go; rates measure what they charge. In a session where five tankers were destroyed and the IRGC extended its exclusion zone from Hormuz to Chabahar and the Sea of Oman, both fell and rose respectively — fewer ships willing, far higher prices for the ones that go.

Discipline 9, added today: write the invalidation in the same unit as the position. A freight position stops on a freight level. A differential position stops on that differential. A proxy variable is an explanation, never a trigger.

And I am still not re-entering today. A record print on a 6% single-session move, one day after being stopped out, is the textbook setup for turning a controlled loss into an uncontrolled one. Section 9 carries a re-entry trigger written in Worldscale, not in ships.
Discipline 8, priced by Dubai and propane.

Do not cut a position on the shape of the order book when the differential itself has not moved against you. Yesterday I reduced two positions because bids were withdrawn at the close and a specific bid had vanished from the record. Neither differential had fallen. Both then delivered $10.83 and $1.00 respectively in the next session.

A thin book tells you the market is hard to transact in. It does not tell you which way it is going, and on Sept 8 it was thin precisely because participants were waiting for the news that arrived overnight. Book texture belongs in position sizing at entry and in the honest reporting of assessment quality. It does not belong in the conviction column.

🏪 APPEC day three — the numbers behind the narrative
CONTEXT
  • Goldman’s Daan Struyven gave the cleanest two-sided frame of the week. The market expected a 7–9 million b/d deficit after Hormuz; the actual average has been about 3.5 million b/d, because Chinese demand destruction and rerouting absorbed it. And the forecast: “If production in the Middle East is back at prewar levels in the second half of next year, Brent futures would point to prices of about $80/barrel … but if production is still 4 million b/d below prewar levels, Brent could surge past $120/b.”
  • China’s crude imports fell from 12.58 million b/d in February to 7.15 million in June, recovering to 8.97 million in August. PetroChina’s Wu Qiunan attributes it to high fuel prices, EV penetration, public transport and weak refining economics.
  • Goldman estimates Gulf crude exports have recovered to ~70% of prewar levels including dark-fleet movements, while product exports remain far below. “Product markets are significantly tighter than crude markets.”
  • Abaxx’s Jeff Currie: “The market is underpricing the security premium.” Muted crude response reflects an expectation the conflict ends quickly and a ~4 million b/d surplus emerges next year — but 6–7 million b/d is currently disrupted and may not restore smoothly.

Method. Directional calls separate flat price from cracks, time spreads, cash differentials and freight, then cross-check each against three independent evidence classes: MOC price formation, realisations over time on completed tenders and sales, and physical logistics. Where those disagree the call is split by leg, sized smaller in both legs, or declined entirely — never averaged. Eleven disciplines are now in force, each added after it cost money: (1) name the invalidation on the instrument actually traded; (2) a completed sale realisation outranks the shape of the forward curve; (3) one session in a direction is not a signal, in either direction; (4) every top-five position must cite a dated physical event, a completed realisation or a named counterparty action — never price action alone; (5) a single dated event buys one notch and a mid-table ranking — never two notches, never a top-three slot, never a new position at size; (6) mark the print, not the memory; (7) write invalidations as a single sufficient condition or two joined by OR — never AND — and where a thesis concerns a complex, name the complex rather than one operator’s units; (8) do not cut a position on the shape of the order book when the differential itself has not moved against you — a thin book is a liquidity observation, not a directional signal; (9) write the invalidation in the same unit as the position — a freight position stops on a freight level, never on a proxy variable; (10) a stop that has to be overridden by judgment twice in one week is mis-levelled, not unlucky; (11) before pricing an outage, verify the asset was running.
