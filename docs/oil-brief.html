1  |  Aramco Said Two Things That Cannot Both Be Comfortable
The world's largest producer told a London conference that the supply crisis runs two years deep, and on the same day cut the price it charges Asia by $3–$5 a barrel. Crude fell. The fuel oils rose $15–18/mt. Dubai cash rose while Brent fell.
🇸🇦 Nasser: "the last major tool in the box"
STRUCTURALLY BULLISH
  • Replenishing inventories while meeting demand could take up to two years, as the world grapples with what Nasser called a major supply crisis resulting from the Middle East conflict.
  • "Existing buffers, including releasing inventories, have cushioned the blow — but they are finite, and emergency reserves cannot fix long-term supply issues."
  • "Most of that came from onshore commercial inventories, which was the last major tool in the box." That is a direct counter to the G7's 100 million barrel release three sessions ago.
  • Aramco estimates less than 10% of inventory is genuinely available — most is pipeline fill, minimum tank levels and volume needed for operations.
  • Saudi infrastructure is intact despite major disruptions since the 28 February attacks; Aramco is restoring operations within days of each strike.
  • Nasser also flagged "pressure on both ends of the barrel to intensify."
💡 This is the most structurally bullish statement in the fortnight, from the single best-informed source, and the market sold off. That is worth sitting with rather than explaining away. The honest reading is that Nasser was describing a two-year inventory problem while the OSP was describing next month's competitive position — and the market trades next month. I have no crude position and will not take one on a conference statement, but the "less than 10% of inventory is available" figure is the kind of structural number that matters far beyond this session.

🛡️ Sanctions, Iran, and the shadow fleet
POLICY
  • Japan sanctioned 33 vessels on 2 October — four MR, two LR1, five Suezmax, 20 Aframax, two general cargo and two ro-ro ships. Those tankers have lifted nearly 42.7 million barrels of crude and products this year, almost all Russian, bound for India, China and Southeast Asia.
  • Every one had already been sanctioned by at least one other Western authority. Japan's measures did not cover LNG carriers — Japan remains a buyer from Sakhalin-2.
  • Iran appointed NIOC chief executive Hamid Bovard as caretaker oil minister on 4 October, following the resignation of Mohsen Paknejad. Iranian crude output fell to 2.2 million b/d in August, per the Platts survey.
  • Russian September crude exports rose 8% month on month despite fresh sanctions.
  • Kuwaiti oil production is back to around 2 million b/d. Turkey and Iraq are in talks on an energy roadmap following a new Iraq–Turkey Pipeline agreement; Kazakhstan has resolved its Dunga oilfield PSA dispute.
💡 A sanctions package where every vessel was already designated elsewhere, and which exempts the trade Japan actually depends on, is a diplomatic signal rather than a supply constraint. Russian exports rose 8% in the same month. I would not change a position on it, and the 42.7 million barrel figure is the useful part — it sizes the shadow fleet these 33 ships represent.
🇰🇷 South Korea — record exports, record cracks
SUPPLY SECURE
  • Gasoil crack spreads hit a record $83/barrel.
  • South Korea has fully secured its October feedstock crude, with Q4 crude arrivals expected to reach at least 90% of pre-war levels, per the Korea Petroleum Association and the trade ministry.
  • August clean product exports to Europe reached a record high, and diesel exports to Australia are up 18% year to date.
  • Korea is Asia's top clean products supplier and net exporter, and is being used to "cover essential fuel shortfalls across the region."
  • Vietnam's PVEP lifted crude and condensate output 30% year on year to 2.35 million mt (63,097 b/d) over January–September — 27 development wells, 98% facility uptime, 700,000 barrels added by well intervention.
  • Australia forecasts a 15% rise in crude imports as exports slow and field output declines.
💡 A record $83/b gasoil crack with Korean supply secured and exports to Europe at a record is the single most bullish refining-margin datapoint in the fortnight — and it sits alongside a G7 diesel release and a Formosa offer of 300,000 barrels. The distillate market is being pulled hard in both directions and I hold nothing in it.
🇪🇺 Europe — jet, the Rhine, and bunker tightness
PHYSICAL
  • European jet is being repriced on the risk of Chinese export bans. A Europe-based jet trader: "It's all positive in jet versus diesel. There is the risk of Chinese export bans. Before the announcement, the market had a pretty homogeneous view that the market would be well-supplied for winter. This will change with the exports from China." Korean volumes may shift from Europe to Asia.
  • Kaub was at 9 cm on 5 October — recovered from zero, but Platts calls it "little improvement, remaining critically low," with forecasts indicating only limited further recovery. Barge movements remain constrained by record-low levels.
  • European gasoil structure is split: the 0.1% NWE cargo differential held flat at a $55/mt discount to ICE LSGO M1, while the 50ppm barge fell $1.25 to a $25.50/mt discount.
  • Rotterdam VLSFO availability is tight — some suppliers cannot offer prompt stems at all. A trader: "We tried buying VLSFO from [a supplier] but they said they have none to sell due to quality issues." Blending component constraints are pushing deliveries into the following week. HSFO described as more manageable; MGO comfortable.
  • North Asia: Busan HSFO deliveries are earliest around 28 October, and Japanese HSFO premiums are rising on tight supply.
💡 The bunker desks are the one place today where the fuel oil rally has a physical story behind it. Rotterdam cannot source prompt VLSFO; Busan HSFO is three weeks out; Japanese premiums are rising. That is a materially better explanation for HSFO +$17.78/mt than the OSP-driven residue argument I gave in section 4 — and I should have read Bunkerwire before writing it.
